    Step 1: Visit your doctor

    Tell your doctor you snore, wake up tired, or stop breathing at night. That doctor can be me.

What you get at the end

What does a better night's sleep mean?

These are the benefits studies have found when people with sleep apnea use CPAP regularly. Results differ from person to person. Using the machine most of the night, most nights, is what makes them show up.

Well proven

  • Less daytime sleepiness

    The best-proven benefit. People stay awake through the afternoon, the TV, and the drive home.

  • Better mood

    Depression scores fell within six months in a large trial of people with heart disease who used CPAP.

  • Quieter nights for your partner

    Snoring drops sharply. Many couples get back into the same bedroom.

  • Lower blood pressure

    A modest drop on average, and larger for people whose pressure was hard to control.

  • Safer driving

    In studies of regular users, car crashes fell by about two thirds.

  • A better quality of life

    Sleep-related quality of life improves. This is one of the reasons doctors prescribe it.

Likely, especially for regular users

  • Fewer bathroom trips at night

    Untreated apnea makes the body shed fluid at night. Treatment often cuts the trips.

  • Fewer morning headaches

    Low oxygen overnight is a common cause. Steady breathing usually ends them.

  • Clearer thinking

    Concentration and memory tend to improve, especially when sleepiness was the problem.

  • A steadier heart rhythm

    In people with atrial fibrillation who use CPAP regularly, the rhythm problem comes back less often.

  • Less nighttime heartburn

    Reflux at night improves for many people once the airway stays open.

  • Lower heart risk, for regular users

    Trials are not settled. Studies of people who use CPAP most of the night point to lower risk. Using it matters.

Sources: American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline on PAP therapy (2019); the SAVE trial, New England Journal of Medicine (2016); meta-analysis of crash risk before and after CPAP, SLEEP (2010); reviews of CPAP and atrial fibrillation, blood pressure, and nocturia.
